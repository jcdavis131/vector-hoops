Chapter 0 · Live

Start here in 60 seconds

Forget the acronyms for a minute. Basketball is five people trying to score more points, on more trips down the floor, than the other five. Everything in this guide is a way of answering one of three questions: who played like this, when did this happen before, and what changed?

  1. Pick a player. Search the atlas for any player-season since 1996–97.
  2. Read the map. Seasons that played alike sit near each other. Distance on the map means similarity of play, not quality.
  3. Meet the twins. The model returns the three seasons that play most alike. That is the whole trick of Vector Hoops, and the whole guide is built around it.

Try it now — three doors, no setup

Open the atlas and search for a player you know. Then play Daily Court: five past All-Stars, find each modern twin. Then browse players by skill profile. If you get stuck on a word, jump to the glossary — that is what it is for.

First question to carry into the map

Who played like your favourite player, twenty years earlier? Hold that question, search the atlas, and let the twins answer it. That is the guide’s whole method: a human question first, the numbers second.

New-fan kit: how to watch

Live follows tonight’s games, Schedule covers seven days, and Pixel Replay replays last night’s game. The exact channel varies by game and broadcaster, so check the Live page for that night rather than memorising a channel list. If a word on a game page confuses you, the glossary is the first place to look.

Chapter 2 · Live

The map: a fingerprint for every season

Every season becomes a 64-number fingerprint. Seasons with similar fingerprints played similar basketball. The model reads 130 features per season to build it, and the serving view is built from 14 era-normalized traits — things like scoring, playmaking, shot mix, rebounding and defence, each measured against that season only, so a player is not punished for playing in a low-scoring era or flattered for playing in a high-scoring one.

How to read this map

First, filters before spectacle. Do not drop a new visitor into 12,966 dots cold. Start with one team, one era, or one role, then widen. Second, nearby is approximate. The picture flattens a high-dimensional fingerprint into two or three dimensions: the league map keeps about 62% of the variance in the 14 traits, and the explorer map keeps only about 27% of the embedding’s variance. So “nearby” on screen is a hint, and the model’s true nearest seasons are computed in the full spaces, not read off the picture. Third, solid means the model’s own answer, dashed means the map’s approximation — the same honesty grammar used across the site.

Closeness, in one sentence. Two seasons are twins when their fingerprints point in nearly the same direction. That closeness score is cosine similarity × 100 — a closeness score, not a percentage of anything.

What the map cannot do

It cannot tell you who is “better”. Two seasons can sit side by side because they played alike while one won a title and the other missed the playoffs. It cannot see effort, coaching, injuries, or locker rooms. And a fused or averaged season — a “Chimera” — never existed as a real season. Those limits are stated in full on the Methods page, including one live caveat: the v5 champion was promoted with its promotion gate waived — see Status.

Chapter 7 · Live

Eras: place any player in history

This chapter borrows its shape from Bob Bellotti’s era taxonomy: the league’s history, told through the players who defined each stage, and the rule changes that created the next one. These are a writer’s labels, not the league’s official periods — and Bellotti names the edge cases himself, including a great player who falls between eras. That honesty is part of the lesson.

EraDefined byWhat changed
MikanGeorge Mikan, Minneapolis LakersThe first dominant big man. The league added goaltending rules and widened the lane from six feet to twelve to slow him down. He still led his teams to the first two titles, in 1949 and 1950, and three more after.
Russell / ChamberlainBill Russell, Wilt ChamberlainDefence and impossible stamina. Russell entered in 1956, won 11 titles in 13 years, and averaged 22.5 rebounds a game. Chamberlain averaged 50.4 points a game in 1961–62 and played over 48 minutes a game that season, overtimes included.
Abdul-Jabbar / Dr. JKareem Abdul-Jabbar, Julius ErvingThe sky hook, then flight. Abdul-Jabbar averaged above 21 points for 17 straight seasons. Erving brought the ABA’s freewheeling athleticism into the NBA after the 1976 merger.
Magic / BirdMagic Johnson, Larry BirdA rivalry that started in the 1979 college final and met three times in four years in the NBA Finals. Showtime pace, cable TV, and new media contracts followed.
JordanMichael JordanSix Bulls titles, and defences that got more physical in response. League scoring fell from 110.2 points a game at the start of his career to 95.6 in 1998.
Shaq / Kobe / DuncanShaquille O’Neal, Kobe Bryant, Tim DuncanPower, isolation, and the low post. O’Neal and Bryant won three straight titles from 2000 to 2002; Duncan’s Spurs won five with ball movement and interior defence.
LeBron / StephLeBron James, Stephen CurryOffence tilted back. Scoring had fallen to 93.4 points a game in 2003–04; the league then outlawed hand-checking in 2004 and added defensive three seconds. Three-point attempts and accuracy rose. Curry made over 41% of his threes in each of his first ten seasons.
SGA / Wembanyama / Jokić / DoncicShai Gilgeous-Alexander, Victor Wembanyama, Nikola Jokić, Luka DoncicAn international era, in Bellotti’s May 2026 framing: all four flag-bearers were born outside the United States. Efficient shooting, corner threes, high screens and rolls, and — lately — slightly less emphasis on threes and more on two-pointers. Bellotti’s own caveat: the quartet had only two combined titles at the time of writing, and only Jokić was over 30. Giannis Antetokounmpo, he notes, falls between this era and the last.

How to use this table: find the player you care about, read across to what changed around them, then go back to the map and filter to that era. A player who looks strange today often looks inevitable once you see the rules they played under.

What this table does not show. How many teams were in the league in each era, and the role players, coaches, and systems that made the stars possible. The rule and style changes in the third column are the non-star forces for each era, but a full team-count and role-player layer is a Phase 2 addition — it is not guessed here.

Chapter 12 · Live

Glossary, methods, and downloads

The house rule, borrowed from Her Hoop Stats: for a sports site, use the fewest acronyms you can. Every term below gets a plain-words meaning first, and the technical name second.

possession
One trip down the floor for one team, ending in a shot, a turnover, or free throws. Counting per possession, not per game, is how you compare a fast team with a slow one fairly.
pace
How many possessions a team uses per game. Fast is not the same as good — see the Spade’s trapezoid lesson in the research notes.
net rating
Points scored minus points allowed, per 100 possessions. Zero is average. Positive wins more often than not.
z-score
How far above the league average: 0 is average, +2 is elite, −2 is a weakness.
era-z
A z-score measured against that season only, so eras do not flatter or punish a player.
twin
A player-season whose 64-number fingerprint points in nearly the same direction as yours. Twins played alike; they did not necessarily win alike.
archetype
One of eight kinds of player in the site’s taxonomy — a role, not a ranking.
NBA
National Basketball Association. The league this guide covers, from 1996–97 to 2025–26 on this site.
foul up three
Fouling on purpose while leading by three points, late in a game, so the other team shoots free throws instead of attempting a three-pointer that could tie the game. It trades two likely points for the chance to stop three.
Elo (Phase 3, not yet live)
A rating system in which a team gains points for beating a strong opponent and loses points for losing to a weak one. The guide’s living Elo page is a Phase 3 build, not a page you can open yet.
pronunciation
How a player’s name is said, in plain phonetics. A full pronunciation and nickname guide for player pages is not on this site yet — it is logged as a Phase 3 item, not guessed here.
Game Score
One number that squeezes a box score together: points + 0.4 × field goals made − 0.7 × field goals attempted − 0.4 × (free throws attempted − free throws made) + 0.7 × offensive rebounds + 0.3 × defensive rebounds + steals + 0.7 × assists + 0.7 × blocks − 0.4 × personal fouls − turnovers. Useful as a box-score summary — and, paired against impact, as a way to spot players whose box score over- or under-states their winning impact.
