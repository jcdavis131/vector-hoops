What does the model see?

The vector space — two views, one frame

Games (fast): 12,966 player-seasons, 1996–97 to 2025–26, from stats.nba.com, per 100 possessions. A season counts if the player cleared both gates: at least 15% of the season’s scheduled games (10–15 GP, clamped) and at least 450 total minutes. For a normal 82-game season that’s 12+ games and 450+ minutes; the shortened seasons scale down — 11 GP in the 72-game COVID seasons, 10 GP in the lockout seasons (1998–99, 2011–12). Each season’s 14 features are z-scored within that season. Player counts site-wide are distinct player IDs — 2,426 (distinct display names: 2,422; a few names belong to two different players).

How does it learn?

MTNN — the model behind every guess

17 towers → residual towers → a 64-d fingerprint, L2-normalized so cosine is similarity → decoded to archetype (8), position (5) and skills. An 18th family, injury, feeds a durability read-out head rather than an input tower: as an input it measured −0.088 test recall, because durability is not a style axis.

Input 130 feats, mask m∈{0,1}
h = cat([x·m, m]) → 2·d_in per family
Tower 17×: 2d_in→160 LN GELU → 160→32 LN + skip ×2 → 32-d (544 total)
+ season_emb 12-d → 556 → 556→256 GELU → 64 L2
Heads: 8 arch / 5 pos / 14 next / 18 skill towers — 12 core + 6 wide (64→16→1)

The old test was too easy — a memorizing model scored 1.0. The current test is leak-free by design — train and test never overlap, so the 1.0 score can’t come back. But “leak-free” covers the test, not the champion: the v5 champion was promoted with its promotion gate waived, and the original run files needed to re-derive its numbers have a lineage gap, disclosed in Status. The full scorecard, the training commands and the promote gate live in the research notebook.

Where does the map lie?

The map

Two maps, both PCA(3), both min-max scaled. The league map (Atlas, Play) flattens the 14 era-normalized stat traits — its three axes keep about 62% of those traits’ variance. The explorer map flattens the model’s 64-d embedding, whose first three axes keep only about 27% of its variance — so on either map, “nearby” is approximate, and the model’s true nearest seasons are computed in the full spaces, not read off the picture. Axes are named from their correlations.

What kinds of players are there?

Archetypes — eight types

k-means with K = 8, in two spaces. The labels on players, dossiers and games come from the frozen game contract — k-means on the 14-d era-z serving vectors, seeded so it reproduces; the MTNN archetype head was trained to predict those labels (top-1 0.970 against the training snapshot). The serving vectors were rebuilt in October 2026 and their k-means re-drawn, so the head and the served labels now agree more loosely — cousins, not copies; where a surface shows a model archetype instead, it uses the head’s own assignment. The Trends stream’s prevalence is computed separately, from a K = 8 clustering of the 64-d MTNN v5 embedding, with the same naming rule (names from distinctive skill z-scores).

  1. Rim protection + offensive glass
  2. Three-point volume (low finishing)
  3. Playmaking + steals
  4. Offensive glass + defensive glass
  5. Three-point volume + shot volume
  6. Three-point volume + three-point accuracy
  7. Scoring volume + shot volume
  8. Rim pressure (FTs) + defensive glass

The three-point archetypes describe season-level specialist shooters: a season lands in one when shooting dominates its statistical shape. Star shooters usually sit in scoring-volume archetypes instead — their shot volume dominates their profile more than their accuracy does.

Where else does the model show up?

Career trails and player dossiers

Every player with two or more seasons gets a career trail: their season points joined in order and drawn over the same map projection you see — rookie → twilight, dotted stretches where a season was missed (injury, retirement, lockout). Trails show movement in style, not improvement — a trail drifting across the map means the player’s statistical shape changed, not that they got better or worse.

Every player dossier is built from the same model behind the map — no hand-written bios, nothing to go stale: a minutes-weighted 14-trait style fingerprint, the player’s archetype and how it moved across their career, their era twin (with the plain-words why), how their game changed from first to last charted season, the five nearest player-seasons in style space, and a plain-words read of where they live on the map. All dossier numbers come from the 14-d serving projection (the transparent era-z style space, not an MTNN output) and the derived research assets; era twins are matched in the 64-d MTNN v5 embedding. “How his game changed” compares first vs last charted season only; traits that moved less than half a standard deviation are left out.

What do the Insights cards claim?

Insights — shareable findings

The Insights page curates seven findings about money and sacrifice: the 2016 cap spike, the most expensive seats in basketball, the max-money cheat code, the biggest invisible sacrifices, Love vs Bosh, the biggest same-team role shrinks, and the hometown discount leaderboard. They are the From the ledger series: salary, tax and ticket findings assembled by hand from named public sources (Spotrac, Hoops Rumors, Basketball-Reference and the others printed on each card), each with its source, sample and window on the card face. A ledger card never borrows the model’s authority. Cards computed from the embedding model arrive as their own series when they ship — nothing on the Insights page advertises them meanwhile.

Every card deep-links (/insights.html#<slug>), exports a 1080×1350 PNG rendered in the browser, and has a crawler page at /insights/<slug> with a pre-rendered unfurl image. The daily spotlight never repeats a card shown in the grid below it.

How does the league change?

League drift — the headlines

Orthogonal Procrustes on consecutive-season shared players (≥ 30). Rotation = mean principal angle of Q against the identity; residual = Frobenius norm after alignment; chained to the 1996 root frame; RᵀR = I.

The biggest rotation: the summer entering 2019–20 (2018–19 → 2019–20) at 12.49°* — the league’s playing style turned further that summer than any other in the window. *The 2019–20 season was COVID-shortened and finished in a bubble, so that rotation may reflect schedule and roster disruption as much as a genuine style shift. Next-largest: 2020–21 → 2021–22 at 11.35°.

Where careers end up, from the same trajectories (n = 1,313 careers with ≥4 charted seasons): 51% of players stay stable specialists, 20% migrate to a neighbouring role, 13% drift, 10% reinvent themselves once, and 5% bloom late (shares rounded). Players with the longest careers are the ones who reinvented — 9.3 charted seasons on average, against 8.3 for everyone else. That gap is confounded by selection: the reinvention label needs at least two seasons on each side of the switch, so short careers can never earn it. These are class means with wide within-class spreads — a pattern, not a prediction about any one player. The steepest single fall is Interior Finishing + Defensive Glass: 16.54% of minutes early in the window to 7.98% late (−8.56 points).

These numbers are what the Trends page charted until October 2026, drawn from the same drift series.

What can’t it do?

Attribution and limits

  • No lineup or on/off data — the model doesn’t know who shared the floor.
  • A midseason move is a TEAM_ID change, not verified trade metadata.
  • Tracking exists only from 2013; earlier seasons are masked, not imputed.
  • Position coverage is high but not total; unlabelled rows are masked, not imputed.
  • Archetype names describe centroid stats, not scouting reports.
  • A Chimera’s fused vector never existed as a real season.
  • Motion and music: the page transitions, the Pixel Replay wipe and the site’s music follow huashu-motion — see the footer colophon. Sound is off until you turn it on in the top bar.
